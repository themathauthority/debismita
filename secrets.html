Secrets

from Little Women by Louisa May Alcott • Prose Study Guide

Standard: ICSE Class 8 Literature
Setting: The March Household
Sections: 11

Background & Synopsis

Before the Extract

Josephine March, known as Jo, lives with her mother and three sisters — Meg, Beth and Amy. Jo is energetic, independent and ambitious, and she loves writing stories. In this extract, Jo secretly finishes two manuscripts and takes them to a newspaper office, which happens to share a building with a dentist. Her nervous pacing outside leads her close friend Laurie to mistakenly believe she has been having her teeth pulled. After Laurie corners her, Jo confesses that she has submitted two stories to a newspaper. Two weeks later, one of her stories — "The Rival Painters" — is published, and Jo reveals her secret authorship to her family's collective pride and joy.

Why This Extract Matters
"Secrets" shows that a dream becomes achievable when courage, determination and hard work are combined. Jo's ambition is not selfish — she wants to become independent and use her talent to help the people she loves. The extract also explores sibling dynamics, the value of true friendship, and how a family's affection turns even a small success into a joyful celebration.

Paragraph-by-Paragraph Explanation

22 Paragraphs

Grouped into 11 logical parts, in the order they appear in the textbook. Click any section header to collapse or expand.

Section Overview

As the October days grow colder and shorter, Jo sits on an old sofa for several hours, writing continuously. When she finally finishes the last page, she signs her name proudly, puts down her pen and says, "There, I've done my best!" She means she has given her best effort, knowing the work may not succeed but satisfied that she has tried her hardest.

Jo then reads her manuscript carefully, makes corrections, adds exclamation points, and ties it with a smart red ribbon — small details that show how seriously she takes her writing and how much pride she takes in presenting it well. Yet she looks at it with a "sober, wistful expression," a mixture of hope, excitement and uncertainty about whether her work will be accepted.

JO MARCH
"There, I've done my best!"
Section Overview

Jo takes a second manuscript from her desk, puts both into her pocket, and quietly goes downstairs, deliberately avoiding noise so that no one in the family notices what she is doing. This is where the real secret begins — Jo is not yet ready to tell her family about her plan.

She puts on her hat and jacket as quietly as possible and, instead of leaving through the front door, climbs out through the back window. She takes a roundabout route to the road and boards a bus into town, looking "very merry and mysterious" — excited about her secret mission, yet careful to keep it hidden.

Section Overview

Jo arrives at a building that houses a newspaper office and, on the same staircase, a dentist's office. She looks up the dirty stairs, stands still, and then suddenly runs back into the street — repeating this "manoeuvre" several times. A young man named Laurie, watching from a window across the street, finds her behaviour amusing.

This scene shows that even a brave and determined girl like Jo can become nervous and embarrassed when facing something important to her. Finally, she gives herself a shake, pulls her hat down over her eyes and climbs the stairs, looking as if she were going to have all her teeth out.

NARRATOR
"This manoeuvre she repeated several times, to the great amusement of a black-eyed young gentleman..."
Section Overview

Ten minutes later, Jo comes running downstairs with a very red face, looking as though she has been through an ordeal. Laurie asks sympathetically, "Did you have a bad time?" and Jo simply replies, "Not very." Laurie assumes she has just come from the dentist.

When Laurie asks how many teeth she had out, Jo doesn't understand at first, then bursts out laughing and says, "There are two which I want to have come out, but I must wait a week." She is actually talking about the two stories she has submitted to the newspaper — but Laurie thinks she means teeth. This mismatch between what each character means creates the scene's central humour.

JO MARCH
"There are two which I want to have come out, but I must wait a week."
Section Overview

Laurie becomes suspicious and badgers Jo with questions until she finally whispers the truth: "I've left two stories with a newspaperman…" She explains that she has given her stories to someone connected with a newspaper and is waiting for his answer. She tells Laurie because she trusts him deeply — their friendship is close, honest and affectionate.

Laurie is thrilled. He throws his hat into the air and jokingly calls Jo "the famous American authoress." Jo immediately hushes him, explaining that she kept it secret because she didn't want her family to be disappointed if she failed — a sign that she is realistic even while chasing an ambitious dream.

LAURIE
"Hurrah for Miss March, the famous American authoress!"
Section Overview

Laurie tells Jo her stories are far better than much of the "rubbish" that gets published, and imagines how wonderful it will be to see her work in print. Jo is delighted, because a friend's genuine praise means more to her than a dozen newspaper puffs.

For the next week or two, Jo behaves strangely — rushing to the door whenever the postman arrives, and exchanging secret signs with Laurie. Her sisters notice this odd behaviour and think the two of them have "lost their wits," not realising that Jo is anxiously waiting to hear whether her stories have been accepted.

Section Overview

One Saturday, Meg sees Laurie chasing Jo around the garden and catching her near the trees. She cannot see what is happening but hears laughter, voices and newspapers flapping — a moment of suspense that hints something important has happened with Jo's stories.

Meg sighs, "She never will behave like a young lady," showing her more conventional outlook, while gentle Beth disagrees and says she hopes Jo never changes. Beth is also quietly, slightly hurt that Jo shared her secret with Laurie rather than with her — a small but telling emotional detail.

Section Overview

Jo enters the room, lies on the sofa and pretends to read a newspaper, deliberately hiding its name from her sisters. When Amy asks her to read a story aloud, Jo agrees and quickly reads "The Rival Painters" — a romantic, rather sad story in which several characters die. She reads very fast, likely out of nervousness, because she knows she is actually reading her own work.

Meg becomes emotionally involved in the tragic tale, commenting on her favourite characters — without yet knowing that Jo is the author. This creates dramatic irony: the reader already knows the truth that the sisters do not.

Section Overview

Beth asks, "Who wrote it?" and Jo's secret can no longer remain hidden. She throws away the paper, her face red with emotion, and loudly announces, "Your sister." Everyone is shocked, and Meg cries, "You?" — this is the climax of the extract, the moment the secret finally breaks.

Amy says critically, "It's very good," but Beth reacts with far greater emotion, crying "I knew it! I knew it!" and running to hug Jo. Beth's reaction shows her strong faith in Jo and genuine happiness at her sister's success, without a trace of jealousy.

JO MARCH
"Your sister."
Section Overview

Meg cannot believe Jo's success until she sees "Miss Josephine March" printed in the newspaper. Amy analyses the story's artistic qualities, Beth skips and sings with excitement, and Mrs March is quietly proud. Jo laughs with tears in her eyes, joking that she might as well be a peacock, since everyone is making such a fuss over her.

The whole family asks questions at once — when the story came, how much she earned, what Father will say, whether Laurie will laugh. The narrator affectionately calls them "foolish, affectionate people," because they turn even a small household achievement into a joyful celebration, simply because they love Jo.

NARRATOR
"...for these foolish, affectionate people made a celebration of every little household joy."
Section Overview

Jo explains that the newspaper editor liked both stories but did not pay beginners — though he agreed to publish them, and promised to pay Jo for her next piece. This is a real turning point: not simply fame, but the first opportunity to earn money through her own talent.

Jo becomes emotional because her greatest wishes — to become independent, to earn money through her own work, to receive the praise of those she loves, and to help her sisters — suddenly feel achievable. The narrator states plainly that this seemed to be "the first step towards that happy end," making this the single most important paragraph in the extract for understanding Jo's true ambition.

NARRATOR
"...for to be independent and earn the praise of those she loved were the dearest wishes of her heart, and this seemed to be the first step towards that happy end."

Character Sketches

6 Characters
J

Jo March

The Aspiring Writer
Ambitious Hardworking Determined Independent

The central character. Jo works patiently on her manuscripts and takes pride in presenting them well. Though nervous about submitting her stories, she overcomes her fear. She keeps her ambition secret so as not to disappoint her family, and her ultimate goal — beyond fame — is to become independent and help her sisters.

L

Laurie

The Loyal Friend
Playful Loyal Encouraging

Jo's close friend. He is observant and curious about her strange behaviour, and creates one of the extract's funniest moments by misunderstanding her visit as a trip to the dentist. Once he learns the truth, he becomes Jo's most enthusiastic supporter and gives her confidence during her anxious wait.

M

Meg March

The Conventional Sister
Caring Traditional Proud

More concerned about proper behaviour, Meg disapproves of Jo's unladylike antics in the garden. Yet she is delighted by Jo's success and can hardly believe it until she sees "Miss Josephine March" in print — showing that her love for Jo outweighs her disapproval.

B

Beth March

The Gentle Sister
Gentle Loyal Unselfish

The most openly affectionate sister. She hopes Jo never changes and is only slightly hurt that Jo confided in Laurie first. When the secret is revealed, Beth reacts with pure, unselfish joy — hugging Jo and celebrating without a trace of envy.

A

Amy March

The Critical Sister
Observant Formal Critical

Concerned with manners and appearances, Amy speaks in a "grown-up tone" and responds to Jo's story with critical, analytical praise ("It's very good") rather than open emotion — yet she is clearly pleased by her sister's achievement.

M

Mrs March

The Loving Mother
Loving Supportive Proud

Though she has a small role in this extract, Mrs March's quiet pride when Jo's story is published forms part of the family's joyful celebration, representing the loving, supportive centre of the March household.

Core Themes

Dreams & Determination

Jo does not simply dream of becoming a writer — she writes, revises, submits her work and risks rejection. Courage, effort and hard work are shown to be essential to making a dream come true.

Family Support

The March family celebrates even a small success wholeheartedly. Their joy shows how encouragement and affection help a person pursue their dreams.

Friendship

Laurie is the first person outside Jo's family to learn her secret. His encouragement matters because he believes in Jo even before she has any proof of success.

Independence

Jo doesn't want success merely for fame — she wants to support herself and help her sisters. This makes her ambition meaningful and responsible, not selfish.

Perseverance

Jo knows her stories may fail, yet she tries anyway. The textbook identifies this as the central lesson: a dream becomes achievable when courage, determination and hard work are combined.

Reference to Context (RTC)

4 Extracts
JO MARCH
"There, I've done my best!"
a) Who said these words, and what had the speaker just done?
Jo March said these words to herself. She had just finished writing the last page of her manuscript, after working continuously for two to three hours on the old sofa.
b) How might she have felt at this point?
A mixture of relief, exhaustion and humble hopefulness — she knew she had given her absolute best effort, even without being certain of success.
c) What does this line reveal about Jo's character?
It shows that Jo is hardworking, serious about her writing and willing to take a chance without expecting automatic success.
Deeper Analysis
This opening line sets the emotional tone for the whole extract: quiet pride mixed with uncertainty. It marks the beginning of Jo's secret journey, before we know what she intends to do with the manuscript.
NARRATOR
"This manoeuvre she repeated several times..."
a) What was this "manoeuvre"?
Approaching the doorway of the building, looking up the dirty stairs, standing still in hesitation, and then rapidly turning to dive back into the street.
b) Why was it repeated, and what happened next?
Jo was gripped by nervousness and lacked the courage to face the publisher. On her third attempt, she gave herself a resolute shake, pulled her hat down over her eyes, climbed the stairs and submitted her stories.
c) Why is this scene considered humorous?
Jo behaves as though she is frightened of the dentist, when she is actually nervous about submitting her stories — and Laurie, watching from across the street, is amused without knowing the real reason.
Deeper Analysis
This scene shows that even a brave, determined character like Jo can be overwhelmed by fear when something matters deeply to her — a realistic touch that makes her more relatable.
JO MARCH
"There are two which I want to have come out, but I must wait a week."
a) To whom did Jo say this, and what did she mean?
Jo said this to Laurie. She meant that she had submitted two stories to a newspaper editor and had to wait a week to find out whether they would be accepted.
b) What did Laurie understand by this, and why?
Laurie thought Jo was referring to two bad teeth being extracted by the dentist in the same building. Her nervous pacing outside and flushed appearance when she came down confirmed his wrong assumption.
c) How does the author create humour in this exchange?
Jo and Laurie are talking about two completely different things at the same time — Laurie thinks "teeth", Jo means "stories" — and this misunderstanding creates the extract's central comic moment.
Deeper Analysis
The building housing both a dentist and a newspaper office is the perfect setup for this misunderstanding, and it allows Alcott to blend humour with the underlying tension of whether Jo's stories will be accepted.
NARRATOR
"...for these foolish, affectionate people made a celebration of every little household joy."
a) Who were "these people", and what were they celebrating?
The March family — Mrs March, Meg, Beth and Amy — celebrating the publication of Jo's first story, "The Rival Painters," under her own name in the newspaper.
b) Why does the author describe them as "foolish"?
The word "foolish" is used with fond irony. It highlights their simple, unpretentious nature — they turn even a small household achievement into a big celebration, purely out of love for Jo.
c) What does this line reveal about the March family?
It shows a warm, affectionate family that does not need grand riches to be happy — their love makes them treat everyday milestones as momentous occasions.
Deeper Analysis
This line encapsulates the theme of family support. Alcott suggests that genuine affection, not material success, is the true measure of a household's happiness.

Important Questions & Answers

Exam Focus
1 Why did Jo keep her writing a secret from her family?
Jo was uncertain whether her stories would succeed. She did not want her family to become excited and then feel disappointed if the newspaper rejected her work, so she preferred to wait until she knew the result. This shows that Jo is realistic and deeply cares about her family's feelings, even while pursuing an ambitious dream.
2 Describe the relationship between Jo and Laurie.
Jo and Laurie share a close, trusting friendship. Laurie is curious about Jo's unusual behaviour and questions her until she reveals her secret. Their bond is also playful — Laurie's misunderstanding about the dentist creates humour — but most importantly, Laurie is genuinely supportive, praising Jo's writing and giving her confidence at a time when she is uncertain of herself.
3 How does the author create humour in the extract?
Humour comes mainly from Laurie's misunderstanding of Jo's visit — he assumes she has been to the dentist, when she is actually talking about her two submitted stories. Jo's strange, repeated pacing outside the building, and the sisters' bewilderment at Jo and Laurie's secret signs, also add to the light-hearted tone.
4 How did Jo's family react when they discovered she had written the story?
The family reacted with great excitement and pride. Meg could hardly believe it until she saw "Miss Josephine March" printed in the newspaper. Beth was overjoyed, ran to hug Jo and declared "I knew it!" Amy critically praised the story's quality, and Mrs March was quietly proud. They are described as "foolish, affectionate people" because they celebrate even a small success wholeheartedly, out of love for Jo.
5 Compare the reactions of Meg, Beth and Amy to Jo's success.
Meg is cautious, needing to see Jo's printed name before believing the news — a sensible, somewhat traditional reaction. Beth reacts with the greatest emotional enthusiasm, hugging Jo and celebrating openly, showing her affectionate nature. Amy is more critical and analytical, commenting on the story's artistic qualities rather than reacting purely with emotion. Together, their different responses make the March family feel realistic.
6 How does the extract show that Jo's ambition is not selfish?
Although Jo's desire to be published might seem like a personal ambition, the ending reveals a deeper motive. She hopes that, over time, her writing will let her earn money, become independent and help support her sisters. Her ambition is therefore connected with responsibility and love, not just fame or personal satisfaction.
7 "It takes courage, determination and hard work to make a dream come true." Explain with reference to Jo.
Jo works hard for hours on her manuscript and carefully revises it. She needs courage to submit her stories, repeatedly walking away from the building before finally climbing the stairs. She is determined to continue despite not knowing the outcome, and when told her future writing may be paid for, she is ready to write more. Her success is therefore the result of effort, courage, patience and determination — not luck alone.
8 Explain: "A friend's praise is always sweeter than a dozen newspaper puffs."
This means that sincere praise from a close friend means more than exaggerated public praise. Laurie's enthusiastic belief in Jo's talent, coming from genuine friendship rather than flattery, gives her real emotional strength while she anxiously waits for the editor's decision.
9 What is the significance of the ending of the extract?
The newspaper's acceptance of Jo's stories, and the editor's promise to pay for her future writing, mark only the first step toward Jo's real dream of independence. Her tears are tears of happiness, gratitude and hope. The ending leaves the reader with a sense of achievement — Jo has not reached her final goal, but has taken an important first step toward it.
10 What is the main idea of the extract "Secrets"?
It takes courage, determination and hard work to make a dream come true. Jo works diligently for hours refining her craft despite self-doubt, overcomes her fear at the publisher's door, and continues even when offered no payment for her first work — proving that dreams require bold initiative and resilience.

Revision & Exam Tips

How to Score Well

  • 1
    Secret's motive: Explain why Jo hides her writing — fear of disappointing her family, not fear of failure itself.
  • 2
    The dentist mix-up: Be ready to explain the humour in Jo and Laurie's misunderstanding, a common exam favourite.
  • 3
    Friendship: Quote Laurie's encouragement when answering questions about Jo and Laurie's bond.
  • 4
    Family reactions: Learn to compare Meg, Beth and Amy's different responses to Jo's success.
  • 5
    The real dream: Always connect Jo's ambition to independence and helping her family — not just fame.
